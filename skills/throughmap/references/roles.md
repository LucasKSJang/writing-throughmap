# Paragraph roles

Each item of the analysis takes one `role`: what it does in the argument, read in context, not what it is about. The page shows the role by its English or Korean name, or by the name in `ui_strings`.

| Key | English | Korean | What it does | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `problem` | Problem | 문제 제기 | States the problem or puzzle the piece takes on. | "Cities plant thousands of street trees every year, yet a third of them die before they give any shade." |
| `thesis` | Thesis | 논지 | States the main claim the piece will defend. | "This essay argues that a city gains more shade by keeping its young trees alive than by planting new ones." |
| `context` | Context | 배경 | Gives background, prior work, definitions or the piece's position among other views. | "A street tree, in this essay, is any tree a city plants in a sidewalk pit or a median." |
| `claim` | Claim | 주장 | Puts forward a sub-claim that moves the argument on. | "Most young street trees die of thirst in their first three summers." |
| `evidence` | Evidence | 근거 | Brings data, findings, sources or quotations that support a claim. | "Of 2,400 young trees tracked in four cities, 31 percent of those left unwatered died within three years, against 8 percent of those watered every week." |
| `example` | Example | 사례 | Works through one case or illustration. | "Take one city that planted 5,000 trees in a single spring and had lost half of them by the third summer." |
| `counter` | Counterpoint | 반론·균형 | Raises an objection, limit, constraint, counterexample or balancing point. | "Planting could still win, critics reply, if watering crews cost more than new saplings." |
| `implication` | Implication | 함의 | Draws out what follows from the claims or evidence before it. | "If thirst kills most young trees, a planting count says little about the shade to come." |
| `framework` | Framework | 틀 | Sets out criteria, conditions, a method or a model that later paragraphs use to judge. | "A tree program succeeds when its trees live ten years, grow a full crown and cost less than their shade is worth." |
| `transition` | Transition | 전환 | Bridges two parts without adding content. | "With planting covered, we can turn to watering." |
| `synthesis` | Synthesis | 종합 | Pulls several earlier points together. | "Early deaths, cheap watering and slow-growing crowns all point the same way." |
| `conclusion` | Conclusion | 결론 | Answers the questions or states the final position. | "A city that waters its young trees will have more shade, sooner, than one that keeps planting." |

## Choosing a role

- Take the item's main move. A paragraph that tells one company's story to show a claim at work is `example`, even if it holds numbers; one that puts forward its own point and mentions a study in passing is `claim`.
- Judge by position as well as wording. The same finding can be `context` in one essay and `evidence` in another, depending on whether a claim leans on it.
- Tables and figures take roles too, most often `evidence`, sometimes `framework` or `synthesis`.
- Keep `transition` for a paragraph that only bridges two parts. A transition needs no thread mark, so a paragraph that adds content takes another role even when it is short.
- A restatement of the thesis near the end usually answers the questions, which makes it `conclusion`.

## Pairs that are easy to confuse

### `claim` or `implication`

Both assert something. A claim puts forward a point that the paragraphs around it go on to support; an implication draws a consequence from support already given. Ask which way the paragraph leans: forward, onto evidence still to come, makes it a claim; back, onto what has been shown ("so", "it follows", "if this holds"), makes it an implication.

### `evidence` or `example`

Both support a claim. Evidence brings data, findings, sources or quotations that speak to the claim in general, such as a pattern across many cases. An example works through one case, a company, a person or a scene, to show the claim at work. A paragraph about one company is an example even with figures in it; a paragraph that sums up a dataset covering many is evidence.

An assertion or a reasoning step is neither, however much a claim leans on it: it is itself a `claim` (or a `framework` when it sets criteria, or an `implication` when it draws on support already given), not `evidence`. A paragraph that a gap calls unsupported cannot be `evidence`.

### `counter` or `context`

Both can describe views other than the author's. Context places the piece among them without pushing against its argument: what others have said, what a term means, where the debate stands. A counter raises something the argument has to deal with: an objection, a limit, a counterexample, a balancing point, whether or not the piece goes on to answer it. Ask whether the author would need to reply; if so, it is a counter.

### `framework` or `context`

Both tend to come early. A framework sets out criteria, conditions, a method or a model, and later paragraphs apply it: they test something against its terms. Context gives background that the reader needs but that nothing is later measured against. Ask whether the body comes back to judge things by it; if so, it is a framework.
