# Replace the weekly status meeting with a written update

To: Product team leads

From: Operations

Date: 3 March

The product team meets every Monday at ten for a status meeting. Eleven people attend, the meeting is booked for an hour, and it usually runs to seventy minutes. This memo recommends replacing it with a written update that each lead posts by Monday noon, and keeping a short meeting only for decisions.

The meeting costs more than it returns. Eleven people for seventy minutes is close to thirteen working hours a week, or about six hundred and fifty hours a year. Most of that time is spent listening to updates that concern two or three people in the room. When we timed four meetings in February, decisions took eleven minutes on average, and the rest went to reports that could have been read.

The team already prefers reading. In the February survey, eight of the eleven leads said they would rather read updates than hear them, and six said they skim the meeting notes afterwards anyway because they missed something while waiting for their turn. A written update also leaves a record that people who were on leave or in another time zone can use.

The main objection is that the meeting is the only time the whole team sees each other, and that problems surface in conversation that nobody would write down. This is a fair concern, but the meeting does not do this job well now. In the four timed meetings, only one problem was raised that was not already in someone's notes, and it was raised after the meeting by the people involved. A fortnightly thirty-minute call with no reports, only open questions, would protect that time better than a weekly hour spent on status.

The change is cheap to try. Each lead would post five lines by Monday noon: what shipped, what is next, what is blocked, what needs a decision, and who should read it. The decision meeting would take the last two items only and would be cancelled when there is nothing to decide.

We propose a six-week trial starting in April, measured by the hours saved, the number of decisions made on time, and a short survey at the end. If decisions slow down or problems go unnoticed, the weekly meeting comes back.
