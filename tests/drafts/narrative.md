# The summer I worked at the lake

The bus left me at the end of a gravel road in the second week of June, with one duffel bag and a letter that said someone would meet me. Nobody did. I walked the last mile to the boathouse with the bag bumping against my leg, listening to the frogs and wondering whether I had the wrong date.

The owner, a tall woman in rubber boots, was on her knees repairing a dock when I arrived. She looked at my shoes, then at the bag, and said the cabin by the pines was mine if I could fix the door. It took me most of the evening. When it finally closed, I sat on the step and watched the light go orange over the water.

My job was to rent out canoes, clean them when they came back, and keep a list of who had gone where. On the first Saturday I lost the list. A family of five was somewhere on the north arm, and I had no idea when they had left. I spent an hour on the end of the dock, pretending to coil rope, until I saw their red canoe come around the point.

By July I knew the lake by its sounds. Wind from the west meant short choppy waves and people coming in early. A still morning meant fishermen before six and a line of kayaks by nine. I learned to tell a nervous paddler from a careless one by the way they stepped into the boat.

There was a boy of about ten who came every day with his grandfather. He never spoke to me, but he watched how I tied the boats, and one afternoon I found every canoe tied with my knot, a little too tight. The grandfather only shrugged and said the boy had been practising on the porch rail.

On my last morning the owner drove me to the bus herself. She did not say much. At the stop she handed me a paper bag with two apples and a page from the rental book, the one with my handwriting on it from the first Saturday, the family of five crossed out in red.

I still have the page. I keep it in the drawer with my passport, and I take it out sometimes when I am about to start something new and have no idea what I am doing.
